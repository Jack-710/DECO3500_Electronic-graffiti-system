# Scaffolding of Interaction Design Education Towards Ethical Design Thinking 
During the design process of public digital graffiti, I was deeply inspired by the concept of "ethical design scaffolding" proposed by Loke and Matthews (2020). 
This research emphasizes that ethical considerations cannot be achieved overnight but rather require a gradual construction in practice: designers should begin with specific privacy and security issues, 
such as whether to allow anonymous participation and how to implement lightweight filtering of inappropriate content; then gradually expand to more complex mid-level governance issues, 
including community self-management mechanisms, ensuring fair participation, and multilingual support; and finally, elevate to the macro-level social impact, 
focusing on the cultural conflicts and social inequalities that digital graffiti may cause in public spaces. This "shallow to deep" ethical scaffolding approach reminds me that digital graffiti is 
not just a tool for artistic interaction; it also reshapes the power relations of expression in public spaces, affecting who can be seen and who may be excluded. Therefore, during the design process, 
I need to both confront potential misconduct and real-world dilemmas, and through community co-management, user incentives, and the setting of value boundaries, 
gradually extend ethical responsibility from designers to users, so that digital graffiti can become a public art platform that is both creative and socially responsible.

# The trouble with social computing systems research
Bernstein et al.'s (2011) critical reflection on social computing research has provided profound insights into my thinking regarding public digital graffiti design. 
The article notes that many studies of social computing systems often focus on short-term experiments, using limited samples and lacking an understanding of long-term usage and sustainability. 
This reminds me that we should not focus solely on the initial user interest and novelty, but rather consider whether the system remains engaging and vibrant weeks or even months later. Furthermore, 
the authors emphasize that academic research rarely reports on failure cases or reasons for user churn, yet these "missing voices" are crucial for improving design. 
This insight has prompted me to recognize that even low engagement or abandoned interaction methods during prototype testing of digital graffiti should be viewed as valuable research findings, 
as they reveal the gap between user needs and design assumptions. More importantly, the article reminds me that truly successful public interactive systems should not rely solely on fleeting novelty 
but rather achieve long-term engagement and dissemination through sustained social incentives, appropriate feedback mechanisms, and the fostering of a sense of community. 
Therefore, when I design public digital graffiti, I not only pursue immediate interactive effects, but also establish mechanisms for long-term use and incorporate failure experiences into reflection on design iterations.

# Do Artifacts Have Politics? 
Winner (1980) argued in "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" that technology is never neutral; its design and form often embody politics and power relations. 
This perspective is crucial for my design of public digital graffiti systems. As an interactive medium in public spaces, 
digital graffiti walls are more than just tools for artistic creation; they also determine who can express themselves, how they can express themselves, and who may be excluded. For example, 
if the system only supports English input, it will implicitly exclude non-English speakers; if the screen height is not designed appropriately, children or people with disabilities may find it difficult to participate; 
if it only supports single-person touch operation, it will reduce the possibility of multi-person collaboration. These design details actually reflect the distribution of social power. Therefore, in my projects, 
I must be aware of the potential political implications of every design choice and mitigate potential inequalities and exclusion by incorporating multilingual support, barrier-free design, and multi-point interaction. 
Winner's argument reminds me that public digital graffiti is not just a product of aesthetics and entertainment, 
but also a technological device with both social and political implications. Its design must directly address and respond to issues of power and equity in public space.
